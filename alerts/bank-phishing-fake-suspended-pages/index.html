Bank phishing warning: fake login pages hide behind “suspended” notices

A newly reported phishing campaign targets banking credentials and makes fraudulent websites appear shut down when investigators return to check them.

By Suncoast Tech Support · Published November 6, 2026 · Threat awareness for small businesses

What happened: Cybersecurity researchers at KELA reported a network of lookalike banking websites designed to steal login details. The scheme is particularly relevant to businesses that use online banking and treasury services to manage payroll, vendor payments, or ACH and wire transfers.

According to the research, the campaign included 248 live lookalike domains impersonating 189 U.S. financial institutions as tracked through October 25, 2026. The impersonated banks were not necessarily breached; criminals created separate websites that copied their appearance.

Why these fake banking pages are unusual

On a first visit, the fraudulent website may display a convincing bank sign-in page, including the bank's logo, company login fields, and a security-check prompt. But a later visit from the same IP address can show a fake account-suspension page instead. That can mislead an employee, a security reviewer, or a takedown service into thinking the malicious site has already been removed.

Important: A webpage displaying a “suspended” notice does not prove it was taken offline or that its earlier login page was safe.

Five precautions for your business

  1. Use your bank's saved website or official app. Do not access banking or treasury portals through unexpected email or text links, even if the message claims an account has been locked or a payment needs immediate approval.
  2. Inspect the full website address. Watch for unfamiliar domains that combine your bank's name with terms such as “secure,” “protect,” “cancel,” “authorize,” or “support.” A padlock icon alone does not establish legitimacy.
  3. Verify urgent transfer requests through a trusted channel. Call your bank using the number on an existing statement or official app, rather than a phone number shown in the message.
  4. Protect online banking access. Enable the strongest multifactor authentication your bank offers, limit financial permissions, and require appropriate second-person approval for sensitive payments.
  5. Report suspicious banking links rather than testing them repeatedly. Preserve the message and URL, but do not enter credentials or ask coworkers to open it. Your bank's fraud team and your IT provider can investigate using safer methods.

If you entered credentials or approved a transaction: Contact the bank's fraud department immediately using an independently verified number. Ask it to secure online banking access and review pending payments or transfers. From a trusted device, change potentially exposed passwords, revoke unauthorized access where possible, and notify your IT provider. If funds may have been stolen, report the incident promptly to the FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3).
