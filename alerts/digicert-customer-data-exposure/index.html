DigiCert customer data exposure: what small businesses should know

DigiCert has reported unauthorized access to archived customer contact information and, for some customers, identity documents. Here is what business owners should verify.

By Suncoast Tech Support · Published November 10, 2026 · Small-business security awareness

What happened: DigiCert, a provider of digital certificates and related trust services, disclosed that an internal archive containing customer information was exposed following a February 2025 server migration. The system, which previously required VPN access, became accessible from the internet. Attackers were able to register an account and read a subset of archived records from June 27 through July 2, 2026.

According to DigiCert's public incident report, most exposed records contained names, business email addresses, phone numbers, and business addresses. A small minority of customer records also included a submitted identity document, such as a driver's license, passport, or utility bill.

Important distinction: DigiCert says its certificate authority systems, CertCentral, DigiCert ONE, customer login credentials, and issued certificates were not affected. Based on the disclosed incident, customers do not need to replace otherwise valid certificates solely because of this breach.

Why this matters to small businesses

Business contact details can help criminals craft convincing messages that impersonate a certificate provider, IT vendor, or employee. If identity documents were included, affected individuals may have additional privacy concerns. The incident also illustrates how infrastructure changes can accidentally expose a formerly private system to the public internet.

Five practical steps for business owners

  1. Confirm whether your organization received a notice. If you use DigiCert, check your normal account channel and any notification from the company. Do not assume every DigiCert customer was affected.
  2. Verify breach communications independently. Do not sign in through unexpected email links or send documents in response to an unsolicited message. Contact DigiCert using contact information obtained from its official website or your existing account records.
  3. Be alert for targeted phishing. Remind staff to question unexpected certificate renewal notices, invoices, requests for identity documents, or password-reset instructions that reference their company details.
  4. Review protections if personal documents were involved. Follow the individualized notification and consider appropriate identity-protection steps for anyone whose identification was exposed.
  5. Review your own change-management practices. After cloud, firewall, or server migrations, verify from outside the network that internal dashboards and registration endpoints are not publicly reachable; enforce permissions and MFA at the application level, not only through network location.
